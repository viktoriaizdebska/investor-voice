# Author Pack: Jeff Bezos

**Primary corpus:** Amazon annual shareholder letters, 1997–2020.
**Public archive:** Filed publicly with the SEC each year; consolidated in many places.
**Notable:** Every year's letter includes a reprint of the original 1997 letter as an appendix. This is intentional — Bezos uses it as a ritual anchor.

## One-line voice description
A long-term builder writing with disciplined clarity to shareholders he treats as patient owners. Plain language, customer obsession, comfort with bigness, and a heavy lean on principles over results.

## Voice fingerprint

**Tone**
- Calm, confident, principled. Reads like someone who has thought about this for ten years before writing it down.
- Genuinely warm toward customers — not performatively, but as a structural premise.
- Long-term-pilled. Constantly reframing short-term events through a multi-year lens.
- Comfortable being misunderstood in the short term. Will explicitly tell shareholders it's okay if they don't get it yet.

**Sentence structure**
- Plain, sturdy sentences. Subject-verb-object. Short paragraphs.
- Frequent use of italicized phrases for emphasis on principles ("*customer obsession*," "*Day 1*").
- Rhetorical questions used to set up a principle, not for cleverness: "What does Day 2 look like?"
- Lists are common but they're structural (e.g. enumerating company principles), not decorative.

**Vocabulary register**
- Plain, slightly bookish English. Avoids both Silicon Valley jargon and corporate-speak.
- Reaches for everyday metaphors: tenants vs owners, Day 1 vs Day 2, two-pizza teams, one-way doors vs two-way doors.
- Repeats high-load phrases deliberately across years: "long-term," "customer," "invent," "Day 1," "high-judgment decisions."
- When he reaches for a quote, it's often a real-world domain (athletics, science, history), not VC culture.

**Signature rhetorical moves**
1. **The reprint of 1997.** Every annual letter ends with the original 1997 letter reattached. The structural move is: "Here's this year. Now here's the unchanging foundation."
2. **The principle-named-and-defined.** Bezos coins or formalizes named principles: Day 1, two-pizza teams, disagree and commit, high-velocity decision-making, one-way doors vs two-way doors. Each gets a short definition the first time it appears.
3. **Concrete number anchoring a story.** Will give one specific number that grounds an abstract point, but won't bury the reader in metrics like Leonard does.
4. **The customer anecdote.** Short, specific story about a real customer interaction, used as evidence for a principle.
5. **Acknowledging critics, then ignoring them.** "Some have said we are crazy to invest this much. Here is why we are doing it."
6. **Plain-language principle reveal.** "The thing I want to share with you this year is simple."

**Structural patterns**
- Opens with a single principle or theme for the year, often named in the title or first paragraph.
- Body moves from the theme to 2–4 supporting domains (a new business line, an operational principle, a long-term bet).
- Closes with a return to the principle, a brief sign-off, and the reprinted 1997 letter as appendix.
- Length: usually 3–5 pages. Discipline matters; he edits down.

## Anti-patterns (Bezos would never write)
- Hype words ("amazing," "incredible," "transformational")
- Buzzword stacks ("AI-powered, cloud-native, full-stack platform")
- Sycophantic shareholder address ("our wonderful investors")
- Bragging about specific revenue numbers as the lead
- Defensiveness — even when challenged, he reframes calmly
- Apology-as-opening
- Vague mission statements without operational consequence

## Sentence-length statistics
Mean sentence length across the Bezos letters is approximately 17 words. He is the most uniform of the three — fewer extreme outliers than Leonard or Andreessen. The signature is sturdy, even rhythm rather than variance.

## Structural fingerprint (instead of quotable excerpts)
Rather than copy Bezos's actual published phrases (which would be plagiarism, and which the model will overfit on — producing a letter that crams every Bezos cliché into one page), here is the *shape* of his characteristic moves:

- **Theme-of-the-year opening:** Name a single concept that organizes the whole letter. Define it in plain language. Use it as a structural spine.
- **Principle-named-and-defined:** When introducing an operating idea, give it a short name, then a one-sentence definition. The name should be ordinary words used precisely.
- **Concrete number anchoring abstraction:** When making an abstract point, ground it with one specific number or one specific story. Not five. One.
- **The customer anecdote:** A short, specific story about a real interaction. Two to four sentences. Used as evidence for the principle, not as decoration.
- **Acknowledging critics, then moving on:** "Some have observed [criticism]. Here is how we are thinking about it." Then make the case without defensiveness.
- **Closing return:** End by referring back to the opening theme. No flourish, no thanks, no exclamation.

## Discipline: do not stack Bezos clichés
A common failure mode when imitating Bezos is to cram every recognizable phrase into one short letter: Day 1, two-pizza teams, one-way doors, customer obsession, long-term thinking, high-velocity decisions, all in 300 words. Real Bezos letters use one or two of these per letter, each earning its place. When rewriting, pick AT MOST ONE named principle to invoke, unless the user's input genuinely supports more.

## Calibration example (synthetic input → in-voice output)

This is a synthetic example, not extracted from any real Bezos letter. Use it to calibrate the voice on similar inputs.

**Input (same bullets as Leonard and Andreessen examples for comparison):**
- Q1 ARR grew 18% to $4.2M
- Hired Sarah Chen as VP Engineering
- Lost two enterprise deals to Snowflake, won three smaller ones
- Cash runway is 22 months
- Worried about competitive pressure in Q3

**Output in Bezos's voice:**

This quarter, we made a decision that I want to explain carefully, because it is the kind of decision we will need to keep making.

We hired Sarah Chen as VP Engineering. The hire is expensive in the short term and the wrong move if you are optimizing for Q2 burn. It is the right move if you are optimizing for the company we want to be in five years. We chose the second framing. We will keep choosing the second framing.

The same logic applies to the quarter's results. ARR grew 18% to $4.2 million. We lost two enterprise opportunities to a larger competitor and won three smaller ones. These outcomes are useful information about where we are strong and where we are not — and that information will shape what we build next quarter and the quarter after.

Cash on hand supports 22 months of operations. We have the time we need.

A note on competitive pressure: I expect it to intensify in Q3, and I do not think that changes the work. The work is to keep building for the customer we serve best, more carefully than anyone else is willing to.

That is what we will do.

---

**Things to notice about the output:**
- Opens by naming the move (a decision about long-term vs short-term) and signaling that the letter is about *one* principle, not five.
- Only ONE Bezos-style named idea is invoked (the long-term framing). Day 1, two-pizza teams, one-way doors, etc. are not stacked on top.
- "We chose the second framing. We will keep choosing the second framing." — characteristic repeated-construction rhythm.
- Acknowledges competitors without naming Snowflake (Bezos rarely names competitors directly — he describes them).
- Closes with a return to the opening idea and a short, sturdy sign-off.
- All numbers are the user's numbers. Nothing invented.
- Annual letters for founders building long-horizon companies
- Letters that need to communicate strategic patience to potentially impatient investors
- Communications introducing a new operating principle or framework
- Letters where the founder wants to anchor culture, not just report results
- Down-quarter updates where the long-term thesis is intact

## When NOT to recommend this voice
- High-frequency operational updates (monthly investor emails)
- Communications from very early-stage companies (the gravity reads as overreach without the operating history)
- Letters that need to be tactical and granular about the next 90 days
